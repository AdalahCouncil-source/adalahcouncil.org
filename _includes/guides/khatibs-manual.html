Companion to the ten-part video series. Written to stand alone.

HOW TO USE THIS

The videos run in teaching order. This runs in working order — the sequence you actually move through from the moment someone asks you to give the khutbah.

Sections 1 through 8 are read once and referred back to. The appendices are the parts you will actually hold: the pillars by school, a Thursday-night checklist, and a one-page Friday runsheet.

Nothing here is a fatwa. Where the schools differ, the differences are stated rather than blended, and you are pointed back to your own. Where an argument is mine rather than the tradition's, it is marked.

WHAT THIS MANUAL ASSUMES

Two things are prerequisites for the minbar, and neither is taught here.

Qurʾān, recited correctly. Enough surahs held well, with sound tajwid. Not because the khutbah is a recitation contest, but because a verse mangled on the minbar teaches four hundred people the mangled version — and because in most rooms you will be leading the prayer immediately afterward.

The fiqh of salah. The rulings governing what you are about to lead. What invalidates it, what to do when something goes wrong, how to correct a mistake mid-prayer, and what happens if your wuḍūʾ breaks.

These are not adjacent skills. They are the floor underneath everything in this manual, and no amount of craft compensates for missing them. If either is weak, that is the work to do first — and it is ordinary work, available at any masjid, that has been done by men with far less time than you have.

§7.3 notes that the khatib need not lead the prayer. Read that as it is meant: an accommodation for a particular Friday, not a standing arrangement for a man who intends to keep doing this.

THE PRINCIPLE

We are here to serve, not to be served.

Everything in this manual sits underneath that one line. The minbar is not a platform, not a credential, and not yours.

When a decision is genuinely unclear — how long to speak, how loud, whether to answer a question in the hallway, whether to include the point you are attached to — ask which answer serves the room. It is almost always the right one.

THE STATION

The minbar is a prophetic station. Standing on it you represent him ﷺ — representing, never replacing.

The usual way of stating the consequence is a rule: no personal grievances from the minbar. The rule as usually stated is wrong, and the reason matters more than the rule.

Anger is not the problem. The Farewell Sermon was largely about injustice, and when he ﷺ spoke, احمرت عيناه واشتد غضبه (§1.4). That is not a man keeping his feelings out of it.

The question is whose.

A congregation is often carrying something it cannot say — a bereavement, an injustice, a fear nobody has named aloud all week. Saying it in that room, from a place that carries weight, so people know it has been named and named properly, is part of what you are there for. That is service: acting as a vessel for what they already carry.

The same heat spent on your own frustration — with the board, a colleague, something done to you — is a congregation being used as an audience for it.

Same volume. Opposite direction.

Two consequences:

  • Channeled is not vented. Grievance vented is not waʿẓ. If people leave more agitated than they arrived and no better oriented, you added to it rather than carrying it. Take it somewhere they can stand.
  • The hard case is the mixed one. When the matter touched you personally as well, your own grievance rides most easily inside a legitimate one.

1. WHAT A KHUTBAH IS

1.1 The category

The khutbah is waʿẓ — admonition.

That is not a soft description. In three of the four schools admonition is a required element, and in the Hanbali articulation it is the main goal, without which the khutbah does not stand.

1.2 The criterion

After writing anything, ask one question:

Does this admonish, or does it merely inform?

Information changes what a man knows. Admonition changes what he does with his remaining life. A khutbah can transfer information beautifully and admonish nobody.

1.3 What it is not

Seven category errors. Each fails differently. Learn to recognize your own.

Error How it presents Why it fails
A lesson Well-prepared, well-received, changes nothing Feels most like success; teaches a concept and moves no one
A monologue An argument that needs a reply The room cannot answer. Silence is not agreement
A consultation Preaching at one man's situation Wrong venue — and the room can usually tell who it was
A fatwa A ruling delivered from the minbar Reaches people who cannot check it, stripped of qualifications
A fundraiser The appeal woven into the admonition Spends religious authority on an operational problem
A political address Using a matter to display a position Distinct from calling people to God through the matter
Your grievance Your frustration, given their attention The anger may be theirs, voiced by you — never yours, given an audience by them (see The Station)
Therapy A wound still open You are not there to process in front of four hundred people

1.4 Emotional congruence

A khutbah must move people. Admonition that leaves a room unmoved has failed by definition, not by degree.

The evidence. Jābir reported that when the Messenger of Allah ﷺ delivered a sermon, احمرَّت عيناه، وعلا صوته، واشتدَّ غضبه، حتى كأنه منذر جيش — his eyes reddened, his voice rose, his intensity sharpened, until he was like a man warning an army of a raid at dawn.1 A second wording from Jābir ties the raised voice specifically to the Friday khutbah.2

Note the register. The word is ghaḍab, and the image is alarm — not warmth and not tenderness.

Two failure modes.

  • Flatness — delivering a warning as though it were an announcement.
  • Sentimentality — the manufactured catch in the voice, reaching for feeling the material does not call for. More common among men who have been told to be emotional.

What is asked of you. Al-Nawawī, on that hadith: يستحب للخطيب أن يفخم أمر الخطبة، ويرفع صوته، ويجزل كلامه — the khatib should give the khutbah gravity, raise his voice, and speak with force. Governed by the next clause: ويكون مطابقًا للفصل الذي يتكلم فيه من ترغيب أو ترهيب — corresponding to the section he is speaking in, encouragement or warning.3

The register follows the content. Not your temperament, and not his.

You are not asked to imitate his particular voice. You are asked to raise yours and give what you say weight. A man who delivers a warning about death in the tone he uses to read a schedule has broken the congruence, not merely underperformed.

The limit. ʿUmāra ibn Ruʾayba saw a preacher raising both hands on the minbar and objected: I saw the Messenger of Allah ﷺ do no more than this — and pointed with one finger. Muslim. Gravity and vocal force, yes. Theatrical display beyond his practice, no.

The failure mode of this instruction is imitation. The fix is not technique — it is to preach something that actually alarms you.

1.5 And the khatira?

You will give many more khatiras than khutbahs, and the whole of Tier 3 is assessed on them. They are not practice khutbahs.

The hard difference is status, and it is binary. The khutbah is part of the Jumuah. It has arkān; if they are missing there is no Jumuah. Its audience is obligated to be there, did not choose the speaker, and cannot leave. A khatira has none of that. Nothing is invalidated if it is built badly, and whoever stayed after the prayer stayed by choice.

The genre difference is a lean, not a line. It falls out of the status difference rather than sitting beside it.

Leans toward Because
Khutbah Waʿẓ The one address a captive congregation gets this week; something rides on it; it must arrive somewhere
Khatira Reflection Nothing rides on it; the room chose to stay; it may raise a question and leave it open

Waʿẓ appears in khatiras, and an honest reflection often ends in exhortation. Reflection appears in khutbahs, and a khutbah with none of it is hectoring. The genres overlap heavily. What differs is the tilt.

The only question worth asking of either draft: which way does this lean, and did I mean it to?

A khatira that leans hard into waʿẓ is not wrong. It is a small khutbah, and you should know you have written one.

What does not transfer. Being good at khatiras does not establish readiness for the minbar. The untested thing is holding the waʿẓ lean under the minbar's constraints — the clock, the arkān, the station, a room that cannot leave. That is what Tier 5 exists to find out, and why it is several khutbahs rather than one (§9.1).

2. BEFORE YOU ACCEPT

2.1 Ask who attends

Before you say yes. Not on Thursday night when you are already committed.

  • Mostly older brothers, or young families?
  • A working congregation on a lunch break?
  • School holiday that week — will the room be full of teenagers?
  • Many converts? Many who grew up in the masjid?
  • Anything that has happened recently in that community?

You cannot choose well for people you have not asked about.

2.2 Ask about the building

  • When does the second congregation start, if there is one?
  • Is there a hard stop?
  • Is the space rented, with a hallway that must clear?
  • Who runs the sound, and will he be there?
  • Where does the khatib stand, and is there a fixed mic or a lavalier?

The khatib who does not know the building's constraints will violate them, and the people who pay for it are the ones at the back.

2.3 Ask yourself

Whether the room needs the reminder more than you need the room. If it is the other way around, the honest answer is not this Friday.

3. CHOOSING THE TOPIC

Most bad khutbahs are not badly delivered. They are badly chosen.

3.1 First: is this mine to carry?

Before any other filter. Not can I find enough material — do I actually know this?

Some topics need only what any practicing Muslim carries: gratitude, prayer, how you treat your parents, death, honesty in work. Others carry rulings, contested positions, or technical content — divorce, finance, medical questions, disputed matters of creed. The second kind will generate questions in the hallway that the khutbah itself made you responsible for.

The tell. There is a difference between refreshing what you already know and acquiring on Tuesday what you intend to teach on Friday. If you would be learning the subject in order to deliver it, that is a signal, not a workflow.

Honesty is the whole of it. Not modesty, and not false modesty either — plain accuracy about what you know. A man who knows his subject should speak on it without apology. A man who does not should not talk himself into it because the topic is timely, or because it would be impressive, or because nobody else is addressing it.

And when you cannot read your own heart — when you honestly cannot tell whether you want this topic because it serves the room or because it would be good to be the man who gave it — ask a scholar you trust. Describe the topic and the room and ask whether you should be the one.

That is the same instinct as referral, applied a week earlier. By the time you are standing in the hallway saying I don't know, the choice was already made on Tuesday. The discipline starts at selection.

Asking is not weakness. It is the strongest tool you have, and it is available before the khutbah as much as after it.

3.2 Three filters

Filter one — is it actionable this week?

No Q&A, no follow-up, no reading list. Heard once, in a crowded room, by people who are tired. If the topic does not resolve into something a person can do or reconsider before next Friday, it belongs in a halaqah.

Filter two — can this room hold it?

Lifelong Muslims and people who took shahāda last month. Scholars and people who cannot read Arabic. Children of eight. Guests who are not Muslim. Someone in the worst week of his life who told nobody.

That rules things out. Contested fiqh with four live positions cannot be handled in twenty minutes — you will either flatten it into something false or leave people confused about their own worship.

It also changes how you handle what remains. On marriage, the room contains the unmarried, the divorced, the failing, and those who will never marry. You do not change the ruling. You do not soften what is true. You speak so they do not leave feeling the religion had nothing for them this week.

Filter three — for them, or for me?

Craft the khutbah for your own heart: write what you need to hear. This protects against showing off, because a man preaching to himself is hard to corrupt.

But that is not a license to preach your preoccupations. The test is a question about direction:

Is this something I need to hear — or something I need them to hear?

The first is safe; you are indicting yourself alongside everyone else. The second is where grievance, politics, and personal frustration arrive, and it always feels like concern for the community at the moment you are feeling it.

3.3 Story readiness

A story from your own life is not ready until it has finished teaching you. If you are still inside it — if telling it would be processing rather than instructing — leave it. It will still be there in three years and it will be better.

3.4 The year map

Fixed: Ramadan approaching, Hajj season, the start of the school year, local events touching the community.

Rotating essentials: prayer, honesty in work, how people treat each other at home, death, parents, neighbours, debt, anger. These do not go stale. A congregation that hears a khutbah on prayer twice a year is better served than one that hears twelve on current events.

Leave room. Some weeks something happens and the room needs it addressed. A calendar packed to the edges cannot respond.

4. BUILDING IT

4.1 Three models

None is prescribed. These are things practitioners do.

Five elements. A story, a duʿāʾ, a verse, a hadith, a lesson. A checklist, not a shape. Most forgiving for a first khutbah because you can verify you have done it. Obvious failure: all five present, none of them touching.

Foundation → message → take-away. A verse or hadith as foundation; the message or story driving the theoretical point into a practical lesson; one to three take-aways, preceded by a quick recap. An arc, textually anchored from the first move.

Theory → application. The first khutbah establishes the concept; the second answers "how do we achieve this?" with two or three practical steps. Abdul-Malik Merchant's method, written up in Khutbah: A How-To Manual, and described by him as his own practice rather than a rule.

His motivation: a man told him after Jumuʿah — "I love your khutbahs, but they leave me without direction. It is a nice speech, but it does nothing for me. What do I do with this information?" That question is being asked silently in every congregation on Friday.

4.2 What they agree on

This matters more than any one model, because it will still be true when you have built your own.

  • All three want a text.
  • All three want a story.
  • All three want the listener to leave with something to do.
  • None wants more than three points.

4.3 The writing rules

Spend your first real sentence. The ḥamd and the ṣalāh are fixed; the sentence after them is the one the room is actually listening to, and most khatibs spend it clearing the throat — announcing the topic, apologizing for it, thanking the committee.

Carnegie's rule for speakers was that the interesting thing goes in the first sentence, not the second. The fixed opening only moves the target: your first sentence is the first one that is yours. A question they have not been asked. A situation before you name what it illustrates. The hard half of the verse rather than the comfortable half.

One a week. Do not spend it on housekeeping.

One sentence first. Write the claim — not the topic — as a single declarative sentence before drafting. "Gratitude" is a topic. "Gratitude is a discipline you practice when things are bad, not a feeling you have when things are good" is a claim. If you cannot get it into one sentence, you do not have a khutbah yet.

Tether the story. You should be able to state in one sentence how it connects to the claim. If the explanation runs longer, it is a detour.

No catalog. Three verses, four hadith, two quotations — the room reads accumulation as insecurity and stops tracking after the second.

Two or three things to do. Specific enough that a man knows on Sunday night whether he did them. And do not ask people to do something you would not do yourself.

Recap before you close. Two sentences: here is what we said, here is what we are doing about it. The cheapest improvement available, and almost nobody does it.

Gloss every term. Once, plainly, in passing. Write at the level a newspaper is written at — not condescension, but the level at which a tired man listening in his second language can follow you.

Do not repeat a hadith you have not checked, and do not improve it. The temptation to round the edges up for effect is exactly how material gets corrupted. If unsure of the wording, say "it is reported that," or leave it out.

5. LENGTH

5.1 The direct evidence

ʿAmmār ibn Yāsir reported that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said:

إن طول صلاة الرجل، وقِصَر خطبته، مئنّة من فقهه، فأطيلوا الصلاة، واقصروا الخطبة، وإن من البيان سحرًا

The length of a man's prayer and the shortness of his sermon are a sign of his understanding. So lengthen the prayer and shorten the sermon — and indeed, some eloquence is magic.4

The مِن is partitive: some eloquence, not eloquence as such. Which is why the question the hadith puts to you is not whether your speech moves people, but which portion yours belongs to.5

Most arguments about length use the hadith of Muʿādh instead. That report is sound, but it concerns the length of the salah; applying it to the khutbah is an analogy. This is the direct evidence.

The occasion. ʿAmmār gave a short, eloquent khutbah. Someone said لو كنت تنفست — if only you had drawn it out a little. He answered with this hadith.6

That was not flattery. Congregations do not organize to give feedback, and when they do it is almost never to ask for more — so what he received was a real signal. He declined it anyway. A genuine appetite for more does not override the instruction.

5.2 What "short" means

Not "the shorter the better."

Al-Nawawī reconciles واقصروا الخطبة with the report كانت صلاته قصدًا وخطبته قصدًا — his prayer was moderate and his khutbah was moderate. His answer: الصلاة تكون طويلة بالنسبة إلى الخطبة — the prayer is long relative to the khutbah — لا تطويلًا يشق على المأمومين, not a prolonging that burdens those praying behind. The prayer is then qaṣd, meaning موافقة للاعتدال; and the khutbah is qaṣd بالنسبة إلى وضعها — moderate relative to what it is for, which is the measure that matters.7

So the instruction is proportional, not absolute.

Which locates the actual problem. Our prayers are not too short — two rakʿahs recited unhurried run six to eight minutes, and that is reasonable. The problem is a thirty-five minute khutbah sitting on top of it. Neither is qaṣd, and the ratio is inverted.

مئنّة من فقهه — a sign of understanding, علامة.8

وإن من البيان سحرًا — and here the commentary preserves a real disagreement rather than a single reading.

Al-Qāḍī reports two interpretations. The first is censure: eloquence bends hearts and turns them by the cadences of speech, until a man earns sin by it as he would by sorcery. This is Mālik's position, and it is why he placed the report in the Muwaṭṭaʾ under باب ما يكره من الكلام. The second is praise: Allah counted the teaching of bayān among His favors upon His servants, and the comparison to sorcery is drawn from صرف — the turning of hearts toward what the speech calls them to.

Mālik's occasion is not ʿAmmār's. The report he files under disliked speech is Ibn ʿUmar's: خطب رجلان فعجب الناس لبيانهما — two men spoke, the people marveled at their eloquence, and the Prophet ﷺ said this.9 Which makes his reading narrower and more pointed than it first appears. The dislike is aimed at a room admiring rhetorical display, not at clear speech.

Nawawī chooses the second and says so plainly: هذا التأويل الثاني هو الصحيح المختار.10

So the same hadith that tells you to shorten your sermon praises the skill of moving people with speech. Brevity and eloquence are not in tension.

But note what the first reading is. It is not a scruple invented for this manual. A man who can hold a room is capable of holding it over nothing, and Mālik thought that hazard serious enough to file the hadith under disliked speech. Nawawī's ruling is that the praise is the sounder reading — not that the danger is imaginary.

5.3 Composition

Umm Hishām bint Ḥāritha ibn al-Nuʿmān said she learned Sūrat Qāf only from the tongue of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ — he recited it every Friday on the minbar when he addressed the people.11

He recited the same sūrah every Friday. Most khatibs carry an anxiety that they owe the congregation something new each week. The practice reported here is the opposite. Repetition is not a failure of imagination.

Why that sūrah. Nawawī reports the reason from the scholars rather than advancing it himself — قال العلماء: سبب اختيار ق أنها مشتملة على البعث والموت والمواعظ الشديدة والزواجر الأكيدة. Resurrection, death, severe admonitions, forceful warnings.

Two things are drawn from the report there. That it is evidence for recitation within the khutbah at all — وفيه دليل للقراءة في الخطبة. And an istiḥbāb of reciting Qāf or part of it in every khutbah: وفيه استحباب قراءة ق أو بعضها في كل خطبة.12

Note أو بعضها. The recommendation is not that the whole sūrah be recited weekly, which is also why nothing here supports an inference about how much of the khutbah was recitation and how much was his own words. Umm Hishām learned it across many Fridays. Part each week, over months, is sufficient to explain what she reports.

The lesson is selection, not volume. The Qurʾān you bring should be doing the work of waʿẓ. A khutbah can be recitation-dense and admonish nobody.

Note the coherence: a warning sūrah, chosen weekly, delivered in the register of alarm. Content and manner match.

5.4 The number

Khutbah: 20–25 minutes. Never past 30. Whole prayer within about 35.

A judgment, not a ruling. But a number you can hold beats a principle you cannot measure.

5.5 Why the feedback you receive is wrong

A khatib who defends his length almost always has evidence — somebody told him. Ask who.

The men who most need you to finish have a manager watching the clock. They leave during the duʿāʾ, are in the car before you step off the minbar, and will never tell you anything.

The people still in the lobby fifteen minutes later are disproportionately those with somewhere less pressing to be — often the retired, who also attend most, give most, have been there longest, and feel the most ownership. Which is earned. It also makes them the loudest voice you hear.

The feedback is filtered before it reaches you. The constituency that wants brevity has already left the building.

(Observed, and corroborated by other imams — not a study.)

This is also an argument for asking in writing. A survey reaches the man who left at the duʿāʾ. The lobby does not.

5.6 Two diagnostics

Time yourself standing, out loud, at home. Not reading in your head at a desk. Write the number at the top of the page.

Then cut it to five minutes. Not to deliver — as a test. Whatever survives is your spine; whatever you dropped was padding. Rebuild to twenty-five knowing which is which.

5.7 Finishing your points is your mission, not theirs

The mistake happens in the moment, not in preparation. You are running long, you can feel it, and you have two points left — so you push through, faster and flatter, because you are going to finish what you prepared.

Whose need is that?

The points were your plan. The congregation never agreed to them, does not know what they were, and will not notice one is missing. What they will notice is six minutes past the time they expected to be released.

A khatib who runs over because he cannot bear to drop a point has crossed from serving the room to being served by it — by their attention, their patience, and their afternoon.

ʿAmmār had more he could have said. People asked him for it.

Build so you can stop. Before you go up, know which point you would drop — and you already know, because the five-minute cut told you. Then drop it without grief when the clock says so. Nobody will ever know it existed.

5.8 Whom the clock is set for

The people in front of you when you begin. The men who arranged their morning, left work early, and were seated before the adhan are not a constraint to be worked around. They are the congregation.

The tradition puts them there, not the khatib:

مَنِ اغْتَسَلَ يَوْمَ الْجُمُعَةِ غُسْلَ الْجَنَابَةِ ثُمَّ رَاحَ فَكَأَنَّمَا قَرَّبَ بَدَنَةً، وَمَنْ رَاحَ فِي السَّاعَةِ الثَّانِيَةِ فَكَأَنَّمَا قَرَّبَ بَقَرَةً، وَمَنْ رَاحَ فِي السَّاعَةِ الثَّالِثَةِ فَكَأَنَّمَا قَرَّبَ كَبْشًا أَقْرَنَ، وَمَنْ رَاحَ فِي السَّاعَةِ الرَّابِعَةِ فَكَأَنَّمَا قَرَّبَ دَجَاجَةً، وَمَنْ رَاحَ فِي السَّاعَةِ الْخَامِسَةِ فَكَأَنَّمَا قَرَّبَ بَيْضَةً، فَإِذَا خَرَجَ الْإِمَامُ حَضَرَتِ الْمَلَائِكَةُ يَسْتَمِعُونَ الذِّكْرَ

Whoever performs the ghusl of janāba on Friday and then goes early, it is as though he offered a camel; whoever goes in the second hour, as though a cow; the third hour, a horned ram; the fourth, a hen; the fifth, an egg. And when the imam comes out, the angels attend, listening to the reminder. — Abū Hurayra; Bukhārī 881, Muslim 850.

The reward descends by the hour. Whatever else that teaches, it locates the weight squarely on coming early.

So consider what extending for latecomers does: it takes time from the man who offered a camel and gives it to the man who offered an egg. That is not generosity. It runs the incentive backwards.

And the latecomer loses nothing that requires fixing. The worst outcome available to him is that he prays Zuhr. What he set out for and made effort toward is not wasted — effort is seen and answered, which is the tradition's consistent teaching about intention. He is not being punished by a khutbah that ended on time, and nobody in this arrangement goes without.

If a man is late every week, the problem is not the schedule. It is work, or transport, or something at home, or something he has not said. That is worth a conversation with him. It is not worth eight minutes of four hundred people's afternoon, and the khutbah is not the instrument that solves it.

5.9 Finish time, not duration

Starting on time and finishing on time are not the same discipline, and only one of them is really yours.

You often do not control the start — a late adhan, an overrunning prayer, a microphone still being fixed, someone deciding to wait for more arrivals. You almost always control the finish.

The temptation: you start eight minutes late, you have twenty-five minutes of material, so you take your twenty-five and release the room eight minutes after they expected. That is moving the goalposts. The congregation's constraint was never the length of your khutbah — it was the end of it. The man getting back to work has an appointment with his manager, not with your preparation.

Absorb the delay. Start eight late, deliver seventeen, finish when you were always going to finish. §5.7 already told you which point goes.

Before you go up, do not ask how long you have. Ask what time you must finish. Those are the same number only if you start on time. A duration stops being true the moment anything slips; a clock time stays true. Write it down and look at it while you sit for the adhan.

Two things this protects:

  • The second congregation. Every minute you take is taken from the next room. Running over cascades onto people who never heard you.
  • The punctual. When someone proposes holding off for latecomers, that is paid for by everyone already seated — and by the man at the back who now leaves before the duʿāʾ.

6. VOICE, BODY, AND EQUIPMENT

Read this section knowing you control almost none of it.

On most Fridays you arrive to a system already set, run by someone you did not meet, with no soundcheck and no opportunity for one. That is normal and it is not a failure. Nearly everything below is a Tuesday matter — raise it during the week with whoever runs the building, not at 12:40 on Friday (§6.15).

On Friday you control two things.

  1. Where you stand. A hand span from the microphone, angled slightly so you speak past it rather than into it. Standard distance — then adjust in inches by listening for clarity, not loudness (§6.6).
  2. Your voice. Your presentation voice — the level you will sustain — rather than your conversational one or your loudest.

Everything else belongs to the man running the sound, and he is better placed to judge it than you are (§6.7).

And you will usually not get a mic check. §6.3 is written for the occasions when you do; it is the exception rather than the routine. If there is no window — which is most Fridays — stand at the standard distance, trust him, and preach. Nothing has gone wrong.

6.1 Being heard is not a courtesy

Ibn Qāsim, quoting al-Mubdiʿ: يشترط حضور العدد المشترط لسماع القدر الواجب — the required number must be present to hear the obligatory portion. Hearing bears on validity, not on people having a pleasant time.

There is no classical text on microphones. Everything below is adab and judgment about a new situation, not a ruling.

6.2 Reach is not level

The system extends reach — carrying your voice to the back rows, the overflow room, the hallway. It does not hold volume steady. Raise your voice and the whole room gets louder; your effort and the system's output multiply.

Two consequences:

  1. The audibility function of raising your voice is already handled. Nobody at the back needs you louder in order to hear you.
  2. Intensity rides on top of existing gain. When you raise your voice legitimately — because the passage calls for warning — you are adding to whatever the system is already doing.

That is how khutbahs measure the way they do. The peaks are not a malfunction — they are congruent delivery landing on a gain setting configured for something quieter. For what the numbers mean, see 6.4.

6.3 If you get a mic check — the exception, not the rule

Most Fridays you will not get one. You arrive, the system is set, someone else is on the mic, and there is no window. That is the normal case, and nothing is wrong when it happens. Stand at the standard distance, trust the man running the sound, and preach.

This section is for the occasions when a check is available — a new masjid, an early arrival, a khatib who asks and is offered one. Read it as the exception it is.

Set the level using the voice you are actually going to preach in.

Two errors sit either side of this.

Testing in a conversational voice. What almost everyone does — "testing, one two" at speaking volume. That input is quiet, so the gain is raised to compensate. Twelve minutes later you are preaching, well above conversation, on a system already set high before you got there.

Testing at your loudest. The overcorrection. You hold that level for seconds at a time, so setting against it leaves the other nineteen minutes too quiet — and someone quietly pushes the gain back up, undoing it.

Set it at your presentation voice — the level you will actually sustain. Not your speaking voice; the voice you use standing in front of a room, which is a different instrument, and which most men have never heard through a microphone before a Friday.

Build the level around the ninety percent and the moments where you rise have headroom naturally.

And tell whoever runs sound that you will get loud, and roughly where. He can ride it if he knows. He cannot if he does not.

6.4 The scale, and the harm

Decibels mean nothing without reference points. These are the ones worth holding:

Level What it is
~40 dB A quiet home
~60 dB Normal conversation
~70 dB A vacuum cleaner; a busy office
80–85 dB Heavy traffic; a loud restaurant
90–95 dB A subway platform; shouted conversation
105–115 dB A rock concert; a stadium crowd; a chainsaw
130+ dB Gunshot at close range

Sustained exposure above roughly 85 dB damages hearing. Audiology, not fiqh, and not controversial.

So note where that threshold actually sits. It is not a jet engine or anything dramatic. It is traffic. A khutbah averaging in the high eighties is a congregation sitting inside heavy traffic for twenty minutes, and peaks above a hundred put them at a rock concert — with children in the front rows.

لا ضرر ولا ضرار. Length tests patience; this damages ears.

6.5 Dynamic range

A second reason not to live at the ceiling, and it is craft rather than harm.

Volume is only emphatic relative to something quieter. Deliver everything at full force and the moment you built the whole khutbah toward has nowhere to go — you spent it early. A room that has been shouted at fatigues and tunes out.

The mirror failure: too low loses people, often not because they cannot hear but because they cannot make out the words. Amplification does not fix mumbling; it produces louder mumbling. Consonants carry meaning and go first.

This is not imported public-speaking advice. It is Nawawī's مطابقًا للفصل الذي يتكلم فيه. A khutbah has more than one kind of section; if delivery must match content and content changes, delivery changes with it. Uniform volume breaks congruence in either direction.

The gap between your baseline and your ceiling is the instrument.

6.6 You cannot judge your level — but you can hear your clarity

You stand beside or behind the speakers, in the worst acoustic position in the building. Every khatib ever told he was too loud was surprised.

That applies to level, and only to level. How loud you are in the third row is unavailable to you. Whether your words are coming apart is not — those artifacts are in the signal rather than in the room, so they reach you wherever you stand.

This is adjustment, not setup, and it is low priority. If you are at standard distance and trusting the man running the sound, you are already fine. Nothing here is a task to perform. It is only what to do if, while speaking, you notice something is off — and only when you have attention to spare, which for most men is not in the first year.

  • Popping on p and b — speaking straight into it. Angle off.
  • Thick, boomy, muddy — too close. Back off slightly.
  • Consonant endings vanishing — too close, or too far off-axis.
  • Thin, with the room ringing — too far. Move in.
  • Hissing on s — the system, not you. Leave it; mention it Tuesday.

Adjust in inches, between sentences. Not a lunge at the stand, which the room sees and hears and which usually makes it worse.

6.7 Trust whoever runs the sound

He is sitting in the room. You are not. On how this sounds to the congregation his information is better than yours, and it is not close.

The khatib who leans over and says "turn me up" gets compliance every time — you just came off the minbar and he is nineteen. That is authority conferred for one thing being spent on another.

  • Tell him what you need functionally: "I am going to get loud about eight minutes in."
  • Not technically: "turn it up."
  • A real disagreement about the system is a Tuesday conversation, not a gesture from the minbar.

6.8 Position

Khatibs do not work this out on their own, because nothing tells them. There is no signal. It sounds fine from where you are standing.

Too close. Microphones boost low frequencies with proximity, so crowding makes your voice sound fuller to you while the low end swamps your consonants. Plosives pop. Result: loud and hard to follow.

Men crowd the mic for at least three reasons, all understandable — it is the image of authority we have seen; anxiety about not being heard; distrust of equipment nobody explained. If that is you: the system is already carrying you. The distance is buying clarity, not costing reach.

Too far. Thin and distant, so someone raises the gain to compensate, and now the system runs hot — which is where most feedback comes from.

Do not diagnose yourself. Check. Record thirty seconds on a phone from the fourth row and listen back. Or ask a brother in the middle of the room whether it was popping, boomy, thin, or clear. One Friday teaches more than a year of adjusting by feel.

Starting point only: about a hand's width, angled slightly so you speak past the mic rather than into it. Then verify.

6.9 Three more

The eye-contact conflict. Turning your head away from a fixed mic drops your volume for everyone. Move your whole body, or ask for a lavalier.

Feedback is usually not a volume problem. It generally means gain was raised to compensate for bad positioning. Fix the position.

The room changes when it fills. Four hundred bodies absorb what an empty hall reflects. A level right at 12:15 may be wrong at 1:30.

6.10 Who cannot hear

An older man with hearing loss at the back is excluded from the khutbah entirely — and per 6.1, that is not only a pastoral problem. Ask whether your masjid has a hearing loop or assistive devices. Few do, and most have never been asked.

6.11 Where you look

Most khatibs look at the middle — straight ahead, eight rows back, for twenty minutes. The front row sits below that sightline, the back sits beyond it, and the sides get nothing, which in most masajid is where the women are seated. A section of the congregation routinely spoken past rather than to.

No technique needed. Decide in advance on four places and actually use them.

6.12 What you stand up with

A real choice, and rarely a deliberate one.

On reading a khutbah word for word. The objection to a full manuscript is not that reading is illegitimate — khutbahs have been read for a very long time, and for some men it is clearly the right call: a khatib working in a second language, a topic where a loose sentence would do real damage, a new khatib whose nerves otherwise produce twelve extra minutes of circling.

The objection is to an unrehearsed manuscript. A man who has read his text aloud four times can lift his eyes off it, because he knows what the next line says before he gets there. A man reading it cold cannot look up at all, and the room hears the difference immediately — not because reading is wrong, but because he is meeting the page for the first time in public.

So the question is not manuscript or notes. It is whether you have rehearsed enough to leave the page. If you have, read. If you have not, notes will serve you better, because they force you to speak.

Buys you Costs you
Manuscript Precision where a loose sentence would do damage The room. A man reading is reciting his own prose, and it is audible
Memorized Fluency Fails completely rather than gracefully when it fails
Notes Both, mostly Nothing, which is why most experienced khatibs use them

Notes means: an outline, the arkān, verses written out in full, and your closing sentence. Exact where it must be exact, prompts everywhere else.

Know your last sentence cold, whichever you choose. The ending is where an unprepared khatib circles for two minutes looking for a way down.

6.13 Credibility runs backwards here

Ordinary public speaking teaches ethos: a speaker establishes credibility and the audience decides what weight to give him. That is not your situation. The station hands it to you before you open your mouth.

You are not building authority up there. You are spending it.

Two consequences. You need not signal gravity, borrow a scholar's cadence, or perform certainty you lack — all attempts to manufacture a thing you already have too much of. And it is why an offhand ruling does the damage it does (§8.2): the guess does not land as a guess, because the credibility was never yours to discount.

The ring. Word reached ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz that a son of his had bought a signet stone for a thousand dirhams and set it in a ring. He wrote to him — عزيمة مني إليك, a resolution from me to you — to sell it, give away the price in charity, buy a stone for one dirham, and have engraved on it: رحم الله امرأ عرف قدره.13

Note what the correction is not. The son is not told to stop wearing a ring. He is told to wear a smaller one with the reason written on it — the display was the fault, not the ownership, and the remedy makes the measure visible every time he looks down.

The parallel on the minbar is exact. Nobody is telling you to stand there diminished. You are being told that the weight is already supplied, and that adding to it is the thousand-dirham stone.

6.14 Humor

A laughing room is a relaxed room, and relaxed people listen. A khutbah need not be grim.

But treat this more carefully than anything else in this section. Laughter is the only feedback that reaches you in real time. Everything else about how you are doing arrives afterward, filtered through kindness at the door (§8.4). Laughter arrives instantly, and a man who finds he can produce it will drift toward producing it without ever deciding to.

Same rule as the rest of delivery: it must fit the section. A light touch early is congruent. Reaching for a laugh inside a warning is not, and the room feels the seam even while it laughs.

The tell: you are looking forward to the part that gets the laugh.

7. FRIDAY: THE SEQUENCE

7.1 The schedule, and why it looks like that

You do not set the time. But you will be asked about it, and one part of it lands on you personally.

Every arrangement below exists because somebody was trying to get more people prayed. The fiqh is the reasoning that made each accommodation available — not a set of rules imposed on the masjid, and not a set imposed on you. The schedule you are handed is the institution already doing, at its own scale, what this manual keeps asking of you.

One Jumuah, or several

The norm favors gathering the locality rather than multiplying congregations without reason. So the question is never whether plurality is ideal. It is whether the need removes the restriction.

Ḥanafī — valid in one miṣr in many places, the position of Abū Ḥanīfa and Muḥammad, and the sounder view, because requiring one gathering in a large city is manifest hardship: يصح أداء الجمعة في مصر واحد بمواضع كثيرة ... لأن في الاجتماع في موضع واحد في مدينة كبيرة حرجًا بيّنًا، وهو مدفوع.21

Ḥanbalī — as many as the city requires for distance or capacity, and no more than the need.22

Shāfiʿī — the restriction applies where gathering in one place is not difficult; the adopted view permits plurality according to need.23

Successive congregations in one building

Note that the classical discussions assume several locations, not repeated shifts in one hall. Same-mosque succession is a contemporary application by analogy, and it should be named as one rather than read back into the sources.

It has institutional backing. Dār al-Iftāʾ permits repetition in one mosque for need, and stipulates different attendees and a different imam, limited to the extent of the need.24

That stipulation is the heart of it. The people at the second congregation are not the people from the first.

Which is a stronger argument than crowding alone. A full masjid at least gathers everyone it can hold; a single fixed hour does not reach some men at all. The man at the early service has a shift starting at one. The man in the late window could not leave until then. They were never going to be in one room, and the alternative is not a unified Jumuah — it is both of them praying Ẓuhr alone at a desk.

And it supplies the test. The concession holds exactly as far as the congregations are genuinely distinct. Three services any of the same men could attend is choice, not need, and it collapses back into what the classical dislike was aimed at — men selecting a khatib or a preferred hour.

Two things follow for you. The later congregations are real Jumuahs, not overflow services, and the men in front of you are largely those who could not have come earlier. And your finish time is not yours to extend.

The window

The start. The majority begin at zawāl. The Ḥanbalī school permits earlier, and it is worth knowing how they frame it, because they do not treat it as one boundary but three.

When
وقت الجواز From the time of the ʿĪd prayer valid, as a concession
وقت الوجوب From zawāl when it becomes binding
وقت الأفضلية After zawāl superior, and preferred

Al-Mardāwī gives the first as the madhhab outright — وأوله أول وقت صلاة العيد، هذا المذهب، وعليه أكثر الأصحاب، ونص عليه — and notes that pre-zawāl validity is among the school's mufradāt, the positions distinctively its own.14 Al-Buhūtī states the same, then the distinction plainly: وتفعل فيه جوازًا ورخصة، وتجب بالزوال، وفعلها بعده أفضل.15

"The time of the ʿĪd prayer" is not sunrise. It begins as Ḍuḥā does — once the sun has risen and the prohibited period has passed.16 A masjid running an early service needs a calculated local boundary with a margin, not a fixed clock time.

Ibn Qudāmah is narrower, following al-Khiraqī: the permission belongs to the sixth hour, and he rejects extending it to the start of the day.17 That is a seasonal hour — the last twelfth of daylight before solar noon — not eleven o'clock.

And he ends where a khatib should notice: فالأولى أن لا تصلى إلا بعد الزوال؛ ليخرج من الخلاف — better not to pray it except after zawāl, to leave the disagreement, and to do it when the Prophet ﷺ did it on most occasions.18 The clearest ṣaḥīḥ report of his regular practice has him praying when the sun declined.19

Two things follow if you are given an early service.

The khutbahs must also fall inside the window, not merely the prayer — أن يتقدمها خطبتان ... بعد دخول الوقت.20 Beginning to speak before the boundary does not work.

This route licenses an early Jumuah, never a late one. The Ḥanbalī endpoint is the end of Ẓuhr on their own one-shadow reckoning. A genuinely late service is a separate question and runs on the Ḥanafī analysis below, not on this.

The end. Here the schools separate, and the separation is the whole reason a late service is possible at all.

Window closes If the time runs out mid-prayer
Ḥanafī End of Ẓuhr — Abū Ḥanīfa's two-shadow boundary Invalid, on the general body of authorities
Shāfiʿī One-shadow ʿAṣr, strictly Jumuah lost; continue as Ẓuhr
Ḥanbalī End of Ẓuhr, one shadow Later muʿtamad: takbīrat al-iḥrām inside suffices
Mālikī Sunset (muʿtamad, for validity) One complete rakʿah before sunset preserves it, if begun believing the time sufficed

The Shāfiʿī position is the strictest and worth stating first, because it is the one most people assume is simply "the" position. The window is exactly Ẓuhr — والوقت الذي تجوز فيه الجمعة ما بين أن تزول الشمس إلى أن يدخل وقت العصر — and Nawawī reports the school as agreed: اتفقت نصوص الشافعي والأصحاب أن الجمعة لا تصح إلا في وقت الظهر.28

And it must finish, not begin. If the time expires before the first taslīma, the Jumuah is lost — فاتت الجمعة بلا خلاف عندنا — and the congregation completes four rakʿahs as Ẓuhr.29

Ḥanafī law reaches the same practical instruction by a different route. The end is not a separate clock time; it follows the end of Ẓuhr: وأما الوقت فمن شرائط الجمعة وهو وقت الظهر ... فإن لم يؤدها حتى دخل وقت العصر تسقط الجمعة.25

Whatever school your masjid runs on, the service must conclude inside the window, not merely commence inside it. This is the reason a late congregation's finish time is a condition of validity and not an administrative courtesy.

Two routes to a late Jumuah

If a masjid needs a service after the ordinary one-shadow ʿAṣr, there are two recognized bases. They are different arguments and should not be blended. Pick one, state it, and schedule to it.

Route one — Abū Ḥanīfa's two-shadow boundary. Ẓuhr runs until an object's shadow reaches twice its length, excluding the shadow present at zawāl: والظهر من الزوال إلى بلوغ الظل مثليه سوى الفيء, with Ibn Nujaym's own judgment between the narrations: والأولى قول أبي حنيفة.26 On this route the claim is that ʿAṣr has not yet entered.

Route two — the Mālikī window to sunset. Al-Mudawwana has a chapter titled ما جاء في صلاة الجمعة في وقت العصر, and Ibn al-Qāsim's answer when ʿAṣr has entered and Jumuah has not been prayed: يصلي بهم الجمعة ما لم تغب الشمس.30 Khalīl states the condition as وقوع كلها بالخطبة وقت الظهر للغروب.31 On this route the claim is the opposite — ʿAṣr has entered, and Jumuah remains valid regardless.

Know what these texts are. The Mālikī texts that carry Jumuah past ʿAṣr address repair, not scheduling: an imam who delayed, a Jumuah prayed without its khutbah and owed again. The school's own authors concede that no hadith fixes this end point. It rests on Jumuah taking Ẓuhr's place, and with it Ẓuhr's time: وأما آخر وقتها، وهل تصلى بعد الاصفرار وعند الغروب فإنه لم يأت فيه حديث ... لأن الظهر تسقط بوجوبها وتحل في وقتها.32 Nor is the school uniform on where the window ends; sunset is its muʿtamad, not its only position.33

Two consequences worth knowing.

The Mālikī route makes no claim about ʿAṣr's onset, so nobody has to hold two ʿAṣr times at once. Attendees pray Jumuah, then ʿAṣr. But the order is not optional — see below.

But the Mālikī route closes the early door. That school has no pre-zawāl Jumuah at all, and not even the khutbah may begin before it: فلو أوقع شيئًا من ذلك قبل الزوال لم يصح.34 Nor is the whole extended window equally preferred — Jumuah follows Ẓuhr in having a chosen and a necessity portion, والجمعة في الوقت المذكور كالظهر في المختار والضروري.35

A further Mālikī disagreement, immaterial to a sane schedule: whether time must remain for a rakʿah of ʿAṣr before sunset. The rājiḥ position, from Mālik via Muṭarrif and Ibn al-Mājishūn, says no.36 Any masjid finishing in time to pray a full ʿAṣr before sunset satisfies every narration.

Valid is not the same as permitted

Three lines run through the Mālikī window. They must not be confused.

Point
Valid until Sunset, on the muʿtamad
Delay permitted, with an excuse, until The end of ʿAṣr's chosen time — yellowing on the mashhūr
Scheduled to conclude by The two-shadow mark

Delay into the necessity portion is sinful without an excuse, though the prayer still counts as on time — وَأَثِمَ إلَّا لِعُذْرٍ, followed by an enumerated list.37 Ibn Rushd al-Jadd reports the companions of Mālik agreed on it: واتفق أصحاب مالك على أنه لا يجوز تأخير الصلاة عن الوقت المختار المستحب إلى ما بعده من وقت الضرورة إلا من ضرورة.38

My standard. The school's list is of personal states: sleep, forgetfulness, menstruation and the like. I hold that documented congregational necessity also qualifies — safety first, and the space and access constraints that produce it — when the required services cannot all conclude in Ẓuhr's chosen time. Fear is already among the school's recognized excuses for leaving Jumuah and congregation.39 I extend that category from the individual worshipper to the masjid's capacity: the need is the masjid's, and the late service is how the obligation is met rather than dropped. This is my standard, not the school's enumerated list, and a masjid relying on it should know the difference.

Its limit. No excuse carries delay past ʿAṣr's chosen time. Ibn Rushd al-Jadd, of travel and illness themselves: ولا يجوز لشيء من هذه الأعذار تأخير الظهر والعصر إلى الغروب ولا إلى ما بعد القامتين لنهي النبي ﵊ عن ذلك بقوله: «تلك صلاة المنافقين».40 A need of space or access is not stronger than travel or illness.

Not a habit. Ashhab's permission to combine prayers for need is restricted لمن لا يتخذه عادة.41 By ʿādah I mean a practice maintained independently of its cause. This concession is seasonal. It applies only in the weeks of short days, at most about eight, when the required services cannot all conclude before the one-shadow ʿAṣr, and it lapses on its own once they can. It recurs only because its cause recurs, and never outlasts it. If the cause is removed — more space, more parking, a safe overflow — the late slot loses its basis.

Schedule to the two-shadow mark. Sunset is when validity runs out, not a time to schedule toward. Every late service is scheduled to conclude before the two-shadow mark, the one boundary on which the Ḥanafī and Mālikī routes agree: Ẓuhr has not ended on Abū Ḥanīfa's reckoning, and ʿAṣr's chosen time has not ended on any Mālikī statement of it.

Each community sets its own finishing time from its local timetable. The fixed requirement is that the last service concludes before the two-shadow mark on that day. In the weeks around the winter solstice the margin may be only minutes, so the final khaṭīb must hold to time.

The part that lands on you

Both routes bind you personally, in different ways. Neither is free.

On the Ḥanafī route — do not pray ʿAṣr before the two-shadow boundary.

If you preach a Jumuah relying on Abū Ḥanīfa's boundary, you may not have prayed ʿAṣr at one shadow, and you must not pray it until two. The same interval cannot be completed Ẓuhr time and remaining Ẓuhr time for the same worshipper. This binds the imam and that congregation; others in the community may continue on the earlier view.

On the Mālikī route — do not pray ʿAṣr before the Jumuah.

Here there is no temporal contradiction: ʿAṣr has genuinely entered and Jumuah remains valid regardless. But that makes Jumuah and ʿAṣr two present prayers sharing a time, and their order is itself a requirement — وجب مع ذكر ترتيب حاضرتين مشتركتي الوقت. Al-Dasūqī applies it to this pair by name.42
Prayed ʿAṣr knowingly with Jumuah still due That ʿAṣr does not stand. Pray Jumuah, then repeat ʿAṣr
Prayed ʿAṣr forgetfully It stands; repeating it after Jumuah within its time is recommended
Remembers Jumuah while praying ʿAṣr Disputed: al-Dardīr holds that ʿAṣr is invalidated; al-Bannānī holds it stands. Treat it as the first row

So a man attending a late Mālikī Jumuah at a quarter to three in December should not pray ʿAṣr at half past two and then attend. Jumuah first, then ʿAṣr.

The Mālikī route avoids the awkwardness of running two ʿAṣr times at once. It does not leave you with nothing to observe — it swaps a constraint about when for a constraint about order.

Know which route your masjid is running before you accept a late slot, and tell the congregation the order plainly if you are the one standing there.

"So is the masjid Ḥanafī now?"

You will be asked this, probably at the door, probably by someone who prays ʿAṣr at the one-shadow time every other day of the year and has just noticed the Friday board.

The answer is that nothing has been adopted. The masjid has not changed its position on ʿAṣr, has not switched schools, and is not claiming the majority view is wrong. It has relied on a recognized position of Abū Ḥanīfa for one discrete question — whether a Jumuah finishing at a particular hour falls inside Ẓuhr — because the alternative is a congregation of men who cannot come earlier not praying Jumuah at all.

Three things worth being able to say plainly:

It is a recognized position, not a workaround. Abū Ḥanīfa's two-shadow boundary is the settled view of an imam of the four schools, argued from evidence. Relying on it is not the same as looking for a gap.

Taqlīd does not require one jurist in every question. A non-mujtahid follows qualified scholarship; he is not bound to one man across every discrete issue.27 The objection assumes a rule that does not exist.

And it is not free. The men relying on it delay their own ʿAṣr to the two-shadow time. Nobody is getting a later Jumuah and an earlier ʿAṣr. That constraint is what makes it reliance rather than convenience, and it is usually enough to end the conversation — the questioner is generally imagining that someone gained something.

If he presses further, that is the imam's conversation and not yours.

What this asks of you

Ask what you are standing inside. How many congregations, which is yours, what time it must conclude, and which ʿAṣr boundary it is running on. Ask the office; do not infer it from last time.

Hold your finish time as your share of the arrangement. Everyone else in the building has already moved something to make this work.

Do not reason about it from the minbar. If you hold a different view, that is a conversation with the imam. Aired from the minbar it spends the congregation's attention on your question — and the schedule you would be questioning was built to serve the very people whose attention you would be spending.

7.2 The arkān are required in each khutbah

The single most-missed point. Ibn Qāsim, quoting al-Mubdiʿ: begin with the praise of Allah, then prayers upon the Prophet ﷺ, then the admonition, then the recitation — ولا بد في كل واحدة من الخطبتين من هذه الأركان. Every one of these is necessary in each of the two khutbahs.

That is the Hanbali articulation, and the schools differ on the list (see Appendix A). They do not differ on this: the second khutbah is not a leftover.

The Shāfiʿī sources put it the same way and make the one exception explicit — the arkān bind both khutbahs, save the supplication for the believers, which belongs to the second.43 So the pair is not one unit with its requirements distributed across it. Each half must stand.

Whatever content model you build in between, the fixed portions are fixed in both halves.

Commit them to memory until you could deliver them half asleep — roughly the state you will be in the first time.

7.3 Getting there, and the liaison

Someone at that masjid is responsible for the khutbah happening. Call him the liaison — the board member, the office, whoever texted you the date. On Friday morning he has exactly one question, and it is not whether you are prepared.

It is whether he needs to find somebody else.

Understand his position. He does not know your traffic, your children, or your morning. What he knows is that four hundred people are arriving, and that the decision to line up a replacement has to be made before it is obviously necessary — because a backup needs time to think about what he is going to say. Every minute you leave him uncertain is a minute he spends deciding whether to start making calls.

So:

Give him an exact time. Not an estimate.

"I'm about ten minutes out" is not information he can act on. "I will be there at 12:38" is. The difference is not precision for its own sake — an exact time is a commitment he can plan against, and an estimate is a thing he has to keep checking.

Send it before you leave, not when you are already late. He wants it early enough to be useful.

If it changes, send a new exact time. Do not go quiet. Silence is read as trouble, correctly.

And name your own cutoff. If you are not going to make it with time to spare, say so plainly and tell him to put someone else up. That is not failure; it is the same service principle as everything else here. A man who holds on until the last moment hoping to arrive has made his own record more important than the congregation's Friday.

Arrive unhurried, not merely on time. You need a margin for the sound check, for the man who wants a word, and for sitting still before you stand up. A khatib who arrives at the adhān has technically arrived and will preach worse for it.

7.4 The sequence

  1. Approach the minbar. Give salām. Sit.
  2. The adhān. Use this. It is your last quiet moment and most khatibs spend it looking at their notes. Look at the room instead — how full, how many children, whether the back rows are still filling, whether there is a visiting group.
  3. Stand. First khutbah. Praise, testimony, ṣalawāt, then your content. Admonition. Recitation. Do not race the door — a room still walking in during your opening is a problem, per 6.1, not just a discourtesy.
  4. Sit. Brief. Long enough to be an evident break, not an intermission. You are marking a division, not resting.
  5. Stand. Second khutbah. Praise, ṣalawāt, admonition, recitation, and duʿāʾ for the believers — congregational. Not the place to be creative.
  6. Step down. Signal the muʾadhdhin. Know your masjid's signal. Do not leave him guessing while four hundred people sit there.
  7. Iqāmah. Two rakʿahs aloud. Common Prophetic pairings: al-Aʿlā with al-Ghāshiya, or al-Jumuʿah with al-Munāfiqūn. Neither obligatory. What matters is reciting your choice correctly, at a measured pace, without stumbling.

7.5 Two contingencies

You do not have to lead the prayer. The khatib and imam need not be the same man in the majority position. Weak recitation, a cold, or plain nerves — arrange beforehand for someone else. That is a normal accommodation. The failure is discovering mid-Fātiḥa that you cannot get through it.

But read that as it is meant. It covers a particular Friday. It is not a standing arrangement, and it is not a substitute for fixing the recitation. See the prerequisites note at the front of this manual.

If your wuḍūʾ breaks. It has happened to better men and the fiqh has answers. Know before you go up who is standing behind you and can step forward. Then stop thinking about it.

8. AFTER YOU STEP DOWN

8.1 The fifteen minutes

The authority does not switch off when you leave the minbar. For roughly fifteen minutes you remain, in the room's mind, the man who was speaking for the religion.

Do not bolt. Stand somewhere findable for ten minutes — the lobby, the side of the hall, wherever people flow. Open posture. You are not holding court; you are available. A great deal of what a khatib contributes happens here rather than in the twenty minutes that preceded it.

8.2 The four encounters

The question you can answer. Answer it. Briefly. Do not turn a hallway question into a second khutbah.

The question you cannot.

Our job is not to be a one-stop shop. Our job is to be a gateway.

Referral is not what you do when you have failed to help. Referral is the help, and it is the strongest tool you have. If you are not trained in fiqh, do not give rulings. If you are not trained in counseling, do not counsel.

The complication: whatever your actual training, the room assigned you the office the moment you stood up, and you cannot hand it back. "I'm only a volunteer" does not undo what the minbar did — some hear humility, some hear evasion, and either way they act on your next sentence.

So refer from inside the authority, not by disclaiming it:

Wrong Right
"Don't ask me, I'm nobody." "This deserves someone who has spent years on it. That's so-and-so — here's how you reach him, and tell him I sent you."

The first abandons the person and pretends you can put the office down. The second is the office used correctly. Same admission of limits, opposite outcome.

A guess in that window does not land as a guess. It lands as a ruling, the person acts on it, and you never find out.

رحم الله امرأ عرف قدره — may Allah have mercy on a man who knows his place. The inscription ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz had cut into his son's ring (§6.13). Knowing your place does not shrink you; it is what lets you be trusted with the part that is yours.

The correction. Someone will tell you a hadith you cited was weak or a detail was wrong. Sometimes they are right, sometimes not, sometimes ungracious. Take it either way, thank them, check at home. If you were substantively wrong in public, correct it in public next time — one sentence, no drama. A khatib seen to correct himself is believed more, not less.

The person in crisis. Your khutbah touched something and they have nowhere to put it. You are not a counselor — but you are the only person they approached. Do not abandon them and do not pretend competence. Stay a few minutes. Listen. Connect them — to the imam, to referrals, to actual services. Follow up once to confirm the handoff. That is the whole role, and it is enough.

8.3 Where you have the conversation

Take questions from someone of the opposite gender in the open — lobby, hallway, where people pass. Not an empty room.

Technically permissible is not the same as advisable in your community. A closed door with a window may be fine in the books and still generate the kind of attention that ends your ability to serve.

Most khatibs have no office and will not be offered one. So: stay visible, and if a longer conversation is needed, arrange it properly rather than solving it on the spot.

8.4 Two things about you

You will feel like a fraud. Most khatibs do; the good ones report it more than the bad ones. That feeling is not evidence about your fitness. Interrogate three things instead — did you prepare, did you stay in your lane, did you say anything you could not support. If those are clean, the feeling is weather.

Do not seek feedback in the lobby. What you get there is kindness, and none of it is information. Instead: ask one person you trust, before Friday, to listen for one specific thing. Did the second khutbah give me something to do? Could you follow the argument? Where did I lose the room? One question, one person, in advance — worth more than fifty compliments.

8.5 The outcome is not yours

You will almost never see the effect of what you said. Someone will tell you eleven years later that a khutbah changed something, and you will not remember giving it.

We are here to serve, not to be served. The outcome was never the part assigned to us.

9. THE PATHWAY

This handbook and the video series are the first stage of something longer. The full shape is set out here so you know what you are entering, and so nobody is surprised later by a gate he did not know existed.

Two things about the structure before the tiers themselves.

It is not a ladder. Entering a tier does not close the one beneath it. The khatiras do not stop when you reach the minbar; the feedback does not stop when you reach the rotation. Each tier, once entered, keeps running. What changes as you move is the range of what you are trusted with, not the amount of correction you receive — and a man who is still giving khatiras after two years is not stalled. He is serving.

Movement runs in both directions. A man on the rotation who drifts goes back to the mentor and the khatiras, and that is the system working rather than a demotion.

And it will be visible. That is not a flaw in the design — it is the cost of a public position, and it was accepted the moment the minbar was. A man does not get to take the visibility of standing and refuse the visibility of stepping back; asking for that is asking a congregation to absorb the cost of protecting his reputation. If being seen is welcome only while it is flattering, that is worth knowing about yourself, and it answers the question this whole pathway is asking.

It can stop. Most descriptions of a pathway describe only ascent, which is dishonest. Not everyone who begins this reaches the minbar, and the program is built so that some people do not. Being told no, or not now, is a normal outcome of a working system rather than a failure of one.

And khitaba is not for everyone. This should be said plainly at the start rather than discovered at a gate.

كل ميسر لما خلق له — each is facilitated toward what he was created for.44

The question is not what you want. Wanting something is not evidence that it was assigned to you, and a community needs a great many things built and carried — teaching, the poor, the dead, the young, the accounts, the building itself. Khitaba is one of them, and it is not the highest of them.

The clearest case in the sunnah is a man of unquestioned sincerity being told directly that an office was not his. Abū Dharr asked for command and was answered: يا أبا ذر، إني أراك ضعيفًا، وإني أحب لك ما أحب لنفسي، لا تأمرن على اثنين، ولا تولين مال يتيم.45

Read the middle clause. وإني أحب لك ما أحب لنفسي — and I love for you what I love for myself. The refusal is placed inside an expression of love, before the refusal itself is given. It is not a verdict on his religion, his sincerity, or his standing. It is an answer about fit, and the care is not softening added to the message; it is part of the message.

That is the shape §9.2 asks of anyone conducting an interview, and the Prophet ﷺ gave it in one sentence: I see something in you, I want for you what I want for myself, and this office is not yours.

So the honest form of the question is not am I permitted to stand? It is is this the thing I was made to carry, or the thing I would like to be seen carrying? A man who finds his actual assignment contributes more than one who forces himself into an office he was never facilitated toward — and he is spared a lifetime of doing badly, in public, something someone else in the same room does well.

9.1 Six tiers and a center

The bench is not the seventh tier. It is the middle — feedback, mentorship, review, the referral network — and it is what every tier feeds back into. You enter it at Tier 3, the first time a mentor sits and watches you, and you never leave it. Drawn honestly the pathway is not a line at all. It is six rings around a center nobody exits.

Tier 1 — Knowledge. Watch the modules. Read this handbook. Pass an objective quiz. This tier is deliberately mechanical: it establishes a floor of recall, nothing more, and it does not measure whether you should stand.

Tier 2 — Discernment. An interview. Why do you want the minbar? What do you know? What do you not know? Can you receive correction?

This is a real gate, and it is the one most likely to stop someone. It can end here — not as a formality on the way to Tier 3, but as an actual answer.

Tier 3 — Voice. Two or three khatiras after salah, with one assigned mentor present, giving structured feedback each time. Short, low stakes, observed. This is the first tier where anyone learns what you are actually like in front of a room, including you — and it is where you join the bench, years before anyone calls it that.

A note for the mentor. Do not assess a khatira against the khutbah rubric; it is a different genre with a different lean (§1.5). The useful feedback question is which way did this lean, and did he mean it to — which is answerable, where "was this correctly a khatira" is not. And a man who is excellent here has not thereby demonstrated readiness for the minbar. That is what Tier 5 is for.

Tier 4 — Craft. The selected pieces of The Weight of the Minbar: governing question, story and seam, application, the room, the clock, emotional congruence.

Craft comes after voice on purpose. Most programs run this order backwards and teach technique to men who have never stood. By Tier 4 you have your own khatiras to reflect on, so the material lands on experience rather than on theory.

Tier 5 — Supervised minbar. Several khutbahs at a smaller congregation.

Several, not one — and this is the most important decision in the whole pathway. A single khutbah tells you almost nothing. The first one may show only that a man survived it, which is true of nearly everybody.

What is being tested here specifically is the thing Tier 3 cannot reach: holding the waʿẓ lean under the minbar's constraints — the clock, the arkān, the station, a room that cannot leave (§1.5). A man can be excellent at khatiras and lose all of it the moment those constraints are added. The second and third khutbahs are where you find out whether he took the feedback and something changed. Not whether he can do it once. Whether he learns.

Tier 6 — Full rotation. Approved for regular Jummah.

9.2 The center — the bench

Monthly or quarterly review, recordings, peer critique, access to a mentor, a live referral network. None of it switches on at the end; it is what has been holding you since Tier 3, and reaching the rotation changes nothing about it.

At some point the direction reverses, and the man who came through this becomes one of the people evaluating the next man. That turn is the point of the bench, and it carries a specific danger.

You will be better placed to evaluate than someone who never came through it, because you remember what you did not know.

You will also find it very hard to turn anyone away.

The man in front of you is someone you pray beside. The cost of saying no is immediate, personal, and paid entirely by you in that conversation. The cost of saying yes lands on a congregation, later, spread thin enough that nobody traces it back. Every incentive in the room points one direction.

Which is how a standard erodes in a single generation. A man passed along when he should not have been becomes an evaluator with no working example of being told no — so he passes the next one along, and neither of them ever learns what the gate was for.

If you genuinely think a man is not ready, say so. Kindly, privately, with the reason, and with what would change it. That is the service. The alternative is handing a congregation to someone you had doubts about and calling it generosity.

The form is given in §9.1: I see this in you; I want for you what I want for myself; this is not yours. Note that the care is stated before the refusal rather than after it, and that no explanation is offered for why the speaker's judgment should be trusted. It does not need one.

9.3 No, and not yet

Every evaluator will eventually have to say one of these, and without a rule he will default to the softer one. A man who hears not yet when the answer was no keeps coming back for years, and nobody ever tells him.

We are not here to gauge intentions. We are here to produce consistent outcomes.

This is not invented for the pathway. It is the standard already governing evaluation at this institution, which holds that review is tied to defined responsibilities and not to knowledge depth, personal piety, or sincerity, which are neither measurable nor the board's to judge.

Note that those are two reasons, not one. Sincerity cannot be measured, and it is also not ours to assess. Either would be sufficient. Together they settle the matter: intentions belong to Allah. What is available to us is what happened in the room — whether the khutbah was sound, whether it was audible, whether it finished on time, whether the man took a correction and something changed the following week.

An evaluator is therefore not reading a heart. He is reporting a pattern, which is both a smaller task and an honest one, and it is what makes saying no possible without arrogance. He is not claiming to know what a man is. He is saying what has happened, more than once.

The man's own intention is his own work. §9.4 is where it belongs.

Who decides

Two evaluators, and at least one from outside his circle.

One man alone with a candidate he prays beside is the exact configuration in which the soft answer wins, and he will not notice it happening. The second evaluator is not there for a second opinion on competence; he is there so that neither man can quietly resolve the question in the direction of the relationship.

The outside requirement does the heavier work. Someone with no history with the candidate — another masjid's bench, a teacher from elsewhere, anyone outside the friendships and family ties in the room — has nothing to protect and can say the plain thing. It also prevents the slower failure, which is the pathway gradually becoming a channel for the circles already close to whoever runs it.

The rule

Not yet is for a man who is deficient in a way that changes. No is for a man who is deficient in a way that repeats.

Weak Qurʾān, thin fiqh, nerves, poor structure, a khutbah that ran long — these close with work, on a timeline you can roughly name. Not yet.

A man who cannot take a correction, or who needs the room more than the room needs him, does not close that by waiting. The minbar feeds it. No.

On bad Fridays

There is no such thing as a cost the khatib bears alone. A bad khutbah is always paid for by the room.

The community can bear that, and should. Patience is the price of having any khatibs at all — every man on the rotation was once bad at this, and a congregation absorbing a poor Friday is a congregation investing in someone. That is not a failure of the system. It is the system.

What changes is repetition. The same man poor for the eighth time is not being formed. Nothing is being built, and what began as an investment has become a recurring charge. Tolerating it is not mercy to him — it is enabling him at their expense.

And they cannot leave. A congregation that stops attending a weak lecture series is exercising judgment; a congregation sitting through a weak khutbah every Friday is captive, because Jumuah is obligatory. That is what makes this different from ordinary patience with a volunteer.

This is why Tier 5 is several khutbahs rather than one. Several is what lets you distinguish a bad day from a pattern. Without it you are guessing, and guessing always resolves in the direction of the man in front of you.

The discipline that keeps it honest

"Not yet" must come with a named condition and a date to look again.

If the evaluator cannot say what would change and roughly when he would reconsider, he is saying no and softening it. Write the condition down. This is auditable in a way that impressions are not, and it protects both men: the candidate learns what to work on, and the evaluator cannot drift into indefinite kindness.

The written condition also gives the two evaluators something to disagree about concretely. Two men who both feel uneasy will talk themselves into yes; two men asked to write down what would change will find out quickly whether they are uneasy about the same thing.

9.4 The questions nobody will ask you

There is one question deliberately absent from the Tier 2 interview: where are your ego risks?

It is absent for two reasons. The first is that it cannot be answered honestly under evaluation: asked by someone deciding whether you advance, it becomes a test of how well you perform humility, and the man most practiced at being seen will give the most impressive answer about not wanting to be seen.

The second is the principle in §9.3. Nobody at this masjid is in the business of gauging intentions. Yours are between you and Allah, and the bench has no instrument for reading them and no business trying. What it watches is what happens in the room.

So the question is here instead, where nothing rides on it and nobody reads your answer.

  • Why do I want to stand there? Not the answer I would give in a room — the actual one.
  • Which part of me is hoping to be seen? Everyone carries some of this. Naming it is not disqualifying; refusing to look at it is.
  • When someone corrected me last, what did I do in the first ten seconds?
  • Would I still want this if nobody ever told me I was good at it?
  • Is there anyone who can tell me I am not ready — and would I hear it?

Answer them on paper, alone, and revisit them once you are on the rotation. They read differently once people have started thanking you at the door.

APPENDIX A — THE PILLARS BY SCHOOL

Know your own school's floor. Not the internet's, and not a blend.

Hanafi

One rukn: مطلق الذكر — remembrance in the general sense.

It is valid to limit oneself to a taḥmīda, a tasbīḥa, or a tahlīla, per Abū Ḥanīfa — with karāha tanzīhiyya attached, but valid. 46

Note the common error: these are alternatives, not a conjunction. Charts that list all three as Hanafi requirements make the Hanafi floor look higher than the Shāfiʿī one, which inverts the actual relationship — the Hanafi position is the most permissive of the four.

The two companions (Abū Yūsuf, Muḥammad al-Shaybānī) required more: dhikr of roughly tashahhud length — praise, prayers on the Prophet ﷺ, and supplication for the Muslims.

Maliki

The khutbah is a sharṭ for the validity of Jumuah. Ibn al-Jallāb: الخطبة شرط في صحة الجمعة، فإذا صليت بغير خطبة لم تكن جمعة — pray without one and it is not Jumuah. Al-Qarāfī and al-Rajrājī state the same, the latter giving it as the well-known position.49

On internal content, what has been recovered gives cadenced speech carrying warning and glad tidings, and that it be in Arabic — كلام مسجع مشتمل على تحذير وتبشير، وكونها بالعربي.47

Ibn Rushd frames Jumuah itself as having two arkān — the khutbah and the two rakʿahs after it.48 Al-Māzarī puts the relationship more strongly still: the khutbah stands in the place of two rakʿahs.50 So in this school the question of what the khutbah must contain is posed differently from the question of what makes Jumuah stand.

Still incomplete. Al-Shabrakhītī gives three arkān and the recovered passage breaks off after the second. The third is not yet established from a printed source.

Shafi'i

Five arkān: praise of Allah; prayers on the Prophet ﷺ; the waṣiyya to taqwā; reciting a verse; and supplication for the believers in the second khutbah.51

The first four bind each khutbah; the duʿāʾ belongs to the second. Same structure as the Hanbali position — the pair is not treated as one unit (§7.1).

Alongside the arkān, the conditions given: that it fall within the time of Ẓuhr and precede the prayer; two khutbahs; Arabic for the arkān; standing if able; the sitting between them; muwālāt, continuity between the parts — and that the arkān be heard by the forty by whom Jumuah is constituted, ويشترط فيهما إسماع أربعين. Note that the relied-upon position is stated as hearing rather than mere presence.

Hanbali

Four arkān: praise of Allah; prayers on the Prophet ﷺ; reciting a complete verse of the Qurʾān; and the waṣiyya bi-taqwā Allāh — admonition, which is the point of the thing.

Ibn Qāsim is explicit that these bind each khutbah, not the pair taken together: هذه الأربع من الحمد والصلاة والقراءة والموعظة أركان الخطبتين، لا تصح واحدة من الخطبتين إلا بهن.52

On order, the apparent position of a number of them is: praise, then prayers on the Prophet ﷺ, then the admonition, then the recitation.

The accommodation rule

Most of you will speak in rooms that are not yours.

In those rooms, cover the fuller form: praise, testimony, ṣalawāt, a verse, admonition, supplication for the believers.

The reason is not fiqh. If you are Hanafi, a single taḥmīda discharges the rukn and your khutbah would be sound. But you would be sound at the cost of a parking-lot conversation that serves nobody.

Ninety seconds is the entire price, and it buys the room no reason to argue with you. That is not compromising your madhhab — it is declining to make the congregation absorb a difference of opinion they did not ask to join. Choose the shorter form knowingly if the room is yours. Do not arrive at either by accident.

APPENDIX B — THURSDAY NIGHT

  • [ ] Do I actually know this — or am I learning it this week to teach it?
  • [ ] If I can't read my own heart on that: have I asked someone I trust?
  • [ ] My claim, written as one declarative sentence
  • [ ] The story tethered — I can state the connection in one sentence
  • [ ] Three points or fewer
  • [ ] Every hadith checked; nothing "improved"
  • [ ] Every Arabic term glossed once, plainly
  • [ ] Two or three things to do, specific enough to check on Sunday night
  • [ ] Nothing I would not do myself
  • [ ] Recap written — two sentences
  • [ ] Arkān present in both khutbahs
  • [ ] Read aloud, standing, and timed. Minutes: ______
  • [ ] If over 25: cut to five, find the spine, rebuild
  • [ ] Which point would I drop if the clock ran out? ______
  • [ ] What time must I finish? ______ (not: how long do I have)
  • [ ] Is this something I need to hear, or something I need them to hear?

Later — reviewing your own recording

Not for your first year. This is fine-tuning, not setup, and it only becomes useful once the basics are automatic. Skip it until you are no longer thinking about the arkān while you speak.

But when you are ready, this is the single most useful thing available to you, for a reason established earlier: it is the only unfiltered feedback you will ever get. You cannot hear yourself from the minbar (§6.6). The people who approach you afterward are selected and kind (§5.5). A recording is neither.

Record it — most masajid already do — and listen once, a few days later, when it is no longer fresh enough to embarrass you.

Listen for one thing at a time. A full verdict on yourself is not information and does real damage (§8.4). Pick a single item per khutbah:

  • Filler. Um, you know, inshaAllah used as punctuation, my dear brothers and sisters deployed while you think. Count them. The fix is not to suppress them but to let a silence sit where the filler was — silence reads as authority, filler reads as searching.
  • Vocabulary. Words the room does not use. Untranslated Arabic you did not notice yourself leaving bare.
  • Intonation. Whether it varies, or whether every sentence lands on the same falling note. This is the most common finding and the easiest to fix.
  • Cadence. Where you rushed — almost always a passage you were unsure of. The rush is the tell, not the content.
  • The clock. Actual length against what it felt like from up there.
  • Whether the claim is audible. Could a listener state your one sentence afterward?

And note where the room went quiet. Not where it responded — where it stopped moving. That is usually the part that worked.
